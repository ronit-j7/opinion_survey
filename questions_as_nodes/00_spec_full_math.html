DPCN Belief Network Spec

This spec covers how we turn the class survey into an item-level belief network: what we estimate, the exact formulas, and which choices are still open. Nothing here has been run yet, apart from the descriptive statistics in §2.

§1Overview

The nodes are the 60 survey statements, not the students.

An edge between two statements is a partial correlation. It measures how strongly two statements are associated once the other 58 are held fixed.

We estimate these edges with a regularized Gaussian graphical model. That is the standard "network psychometrics" approach (Epskamp et al., 2018), applied here to a belief system as in Boutyline & Vaisey (2017).

The network lets us answer three questions:

  • Hubs. Which beliefs are most central to the class's opinion system?
  • Structure. Do the four survey themes come back as four communities, or do opinions organise some other way?
  • Consensus. Which beliefs are held so uniformly that they barely connect to anything?

The pipeline runs in order: clean (§2) → correlate (§3) → estimate the sparse network (§4) → compute centrality (§5) and communities (§6) → test stability (§7).

§2Data and notation

Encoding

Each response goes through the map

\[ \varphi:\ \text{SD}\mapsto 1,\ \text{D}\mapsto 2,\ \text{N}\mapsto 3,\ \text{A}\mapsto 4,\ \text{SA}\mapsto 5,\qquad \text{“No Comments”},\ \text{blank}\ \mapsto\ \texttt{NA}. \]

"No Comments" means the person declined to answer. It is not a neutral opinion, so it becomes missing.

Rows with no observed answers are dropped. That leaves the data matrix

\[ X \in \{1,2,3,4,5,\texttt{NA}\}^{n\times p},\qquad n = 91,\ p = 60 . \]

Let $x_{ij}$ be respondent $i$'s answer to item $j$. Let $M_{ij} = \one[x_{ij}\ \text{observed}]$ be the observation indicator.

Themes

Every item belongs to exactly one survey theme:

\[ \tau : \{1,\dots,p\} \to \{\mathsf{T},\mathsf{E},\mathsf{S},\mathsf{V}\},\qquad |\tau^{-1}(a)| = 15\ \ \forall a . \]
T Technology E Education S Society / ethics V Environment

$\tau$ is only used for evaluation, never during estimation. That is what makes the community-vs-theme comparison in §6 a real test.

Pairwise sample sizes

For each pair of items, define the set of respondents who answered both:

\[ P_{jk} = \{\, i : M_{ij}M_{ik} = 1 \,\},\qquad n_{jk} = |P_{jk}| . \]

Two properties of this table drive most of the choices below.

Missingness grows with position in the survey. The V section has about eight times more missing cells than T. This is consistent with people abandoning the form partway. Listwise deletion would throw away 23 of 91 respondents, so we use pairwise-complete estimation.

Responses pile up at the top of the scale. About 79% of answers are A or SA. Many items have almost no variance. An item with little variance cannot correlate strongly with anything, so it will tend to end up as an isolated node.

Per-item descriptors

We report these alongside the network. They describe consensus, which the network can't show.

\[ \mu_j = \frac{\sum_i M_{ij}x_{ij}}{\sum_i M_{ij}},\qquad \sigma_j^2 = \frac{\sum_i M_{ij}(x_{ij}-\mu_j)^2}{\sum_i M_{ij}-1},\qquad a_j = \frac{\sum_i M_{ij}\one[x_{ij}\ge 4]}{\sum_i M_{ij}} . \]

$a_j$ is the agreement rate. Items with high $a_j$ and low $\sigma_j$ make up the "consensus set".

§3Correlation estimation

The network in §4 needs a $p\times p$ correlation matrix $R$ as input. The data is ordinal, so Pearson correlation on the raw codes is not the right choice. There are two candidates.

3.1 Spearman with the Gaussian-copula transform

For each pair, rank both items within $P_{jk}$, using mid-ranks for ties. Let $\operatorname{rk}$ denote those ranks. Then take the Pearson correlation of the ranks:

\begin{equation} r^{S}_{jk} = \operatorname{corr}_{i\in P_{jk}}\!\big(\operatorname{rk}(x_{ij}),\ \operatorname{rk}(x_{ik})\big). \end{equation}

Spearman's $\rho$ is not a Gaussian correlation. Suppose the data comes from a Gaussian copula, meaning monotone transforms of a multivariate normal. Then the latent Pearson correlation is recovered by (Liu et al., 2012):

\begin{equation}\label{eq:skeptic} \hat\rho_{jk} = 2\sin\!\Big(\frac{\pi}{6}\, r^{S}_{jk}\Big). \end{equation}

The transform is small in practice. For example, $r^S = 0.30$ maps to $\hat\rho \approx 0.313$.

One caveat: \eqref{eq:skeptic} assumes continuous margins. With five categories there are many ties, so the transform is approximate.

3.2 Polychoric correlation

The polychoric model assumes each item is a latent standard normal $Z_j$ cut at thresholds:

\begin{equation} x_{ij} = c \iff t_{j,c-1} \lt Z_{ij} \le t_{j,c},\qquad t_{j,0} = -\infty,\ t_{j,C} = +\infty . \end{equation}

The thresholds come from the item's marginal distribution:

\[ \hat t_{j,c} = \Phi^{-1}\!\Big(\tfrac{1}{n_j}\textstyle\sum_i M_{ij}\,\one[x_{ij}\le c]\Big). \]

With the thresholds fixed, $\rho_{jk}$ maximizes the likelihood of the $C\times C$ contingency table of counts $n_{ab}$:

\begin{equation} \hat\rho_{jk} = \arg\max_{\rho\in(-1,1)} \sum_{a,b} n_{ab}\,\log \pi_{ab}(\rho), \end{equation} \[ \pi_{ab}(\rho) = \Phi_2(t_{j,a},t_{k,b};\rho) - \Phi_2(t_{j,a-1},t_{k,b};\rho) - \Phi_2(t_{j,a},t_{k,b-1};\rho) + \Phi_2(t_{j,a-1},t_{k,b-1};\rho), \]

where $\Phi_2(\cdot,\cdot;\rho)$ is the standard bivariate normal CDF with correlation $\rho$.

Problem · sparse tables

Polychoric estimates are unreliable when contingency tables have many empty cells. The estimate can run to the boundary $\hat\rho\to\pm 1$.

Across all 1,770 item pairs, the average share of empty cells in the $5\times5$ table is 47.1%. A category with zero responses also makes two thresholds equal, so that category has to be removed.

The usual fix is to merge the rare low categories:

\[ \kappa:\ \{1,2\}\mapsto 1,\quad 3\mapsto 2,\quad 4\mapsto 3,\quad 5\mapsto 4 . \]

After merging, the average share of empty cells in the $4\times4$ table drops to 25.1%. That is better, but still high. We would also add a 0.5 continuity correction to empty cells.

3.3 Positive-definiteness

A pairwise-complete matrix is not guaranteed to be positive semi-definite. Each entry is computed on a different subset of respondents.

For our Spearman matrix the smallest eigenvalue is $\lambda_{\min} = 1.3\times10^{-3}$. It is positive, but the matrix is close to singular. The polychoric matrix may well fail the check.

If it fails, we project to the nearest correlation matrix (Higham, 2002):

\begin{equation} \tilde R = \arg\min_{A}\ \|A - R\|_F \quad \text{s.t.}\quad A \succeq \varepsilon I,\ \ \operatorname{diag}(A) = \mathbf 1 . \end{equation}

A cheaper version: eigen-decompose $R = Q\Lambda Q^\top$, clip $\Lambda^+ = \max(\Lambda,\varepsilon)$, rebuild, and rescale to a unit diagonal:

\[ \tilde R = D^{-1/2}\,Q\Lambda^+Q^\top D^{-1/2},\qquad D = \operatorname{diag}(Q\Lambda^+Q^\top). \]
Decision D1 · correlation estimator

Recommendation: use Spearman + \eqref{eq:skeptic} as the primary estimator. Run polychoric on $\kappa$-merged data as a sensitivity check (§7.4).

Reason: polychoric is the textbook choice for Likert data, but our tables are too sparse for it to be stable.

§4Network model: Gaussian graphical model

4.1 Model

Assume the latent item vector satisfies $\mathbf z \sim \mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\Sigma)$ with $\operatorname{diag}(\Sigma)=\mathbf 1$. Let $\Theta = \Sigma^{-1}$ be the precision matrix.

The partial correlation between items $j$ and $k$ is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:pcor} \omega_{jk} = -\frac{\theta_{jk}}{\sqrt{\theta_{jj}\,\theta_{kk}}} = \operatorname{corr}\big(z_j, z_k \given \mathbf z_{-\{j,k\}}\big). \end{equation}

A zero in $\Theta$ has a precise meaning:

\[ \theta_{jk} = 0 \iff z_j \perp\!\!\!\perp z_k \given \mathbf z_{-\{j,k\}} . \]

So a missing edge means conditional independence, not just weak correlation.

4.2 Why regularize

The model has $\binom{60}{2} = 1{,}770$ partial correlations to estimate from 91 respondents.

Inverting $R$ directly would give a dense, noisy $\Theta$. The near-zero $\lambda_{\min}$ from §3.3 would also blow up the inverse.

So we impose sparsity with an $\ell_1$ penalty.

4.3 Graphical lasso

For a penalty $\lambda \ge 0$ (Friedman, Hastie & Tibshirani, 2008):

\begin{equation}\label{eq:glasso} \hat\Theta_\lambda = \arg\max_{\Theta \succ 0}\ \Big\{ \log\det\Theta - \operatorname{tr}(\tilde R\,\Theta) - \lambda \sum_{j\neq k} |\theta_{jk}| \Big\}. \end{equation}

Only off-diagonal entries are penalized.

Implementation check: confirm whether the library also penalizes the diagonal. If it does, the result won't match \eqref{eq:glasso} exactly.

4.4 Choosing $\lambda$ with EBIC

Build a grid of $K = 100$ values, log-spaced from $\lambda_{\max} = \max_{j\neq k}|\tilde r_{jk}|$ down to $0.01\,\lambda_{\max}$.

For each $\lambda$, compute the Gaussian log-likelihood and the edge count:

\[ \ell(\hat\Theta_\lambda) = \frac{n}{2}\Big[\log\det\hat\Theta_\lambda - \operatorname{tr}(\tilde R\,\hat\Theta_\lambda)\Big],\qquad |E_\lambda| = \#\{\, j\lt k : \hat\theta_{jk} \neq 0 \,\}. \]

Then minimize the extended BIC (Foygel & Drton, 2010):

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ebic} \mathrm{EBIC}_\gamma(\lambda) = -2\,\ell(\hat\Theta_\lambda) + |E_\lambda|\log n + 4\gamma\,|E_\lambda|\log p,\qquad \hat\lambda = \arg\min_\lambda \mathrm{EBIC}_\gamma(\lambda). \end{equation}

$\gamma\in[0,1]$ controls how strongly extra edges are penalized. $\gamma = 0$ is ordinary BIC. $\gamma = 0.5$ is the psychometrics default and gives sparser graphs.

Pairwise estimation means there is no single $n$. We plug in the median, $n = 85$. Using $n = 81$ is the conservative alternative.

4.5 The resulting graph

\[ G = (V, E, W),\qquad V = \{1,\dots,60\},\quad E = \{\{j,k\} : \hat\omega_{jk}\neq 0\},\quad W_{jk} = \hat\omega_{jk}. \]

$G$ is undirected, weighted and signed. Its density is

\[ \delta = |E| \big/ \tbinom{p}{2} . \]

4.6 Baseline: marginal correlation network

For comparison, we also build the network Boutyline & Vaisey would draw: an edge wherever the plain correlation is significant.

Test each pair with

\[ t_{jk} = \tilde r_{jk}\sqrt{\frac{n_{jk}-2}{1-\tilde r_{jk}^2}} \ \sim\ t_{\,n_{jk}-2}\ \ \text{under } H_0:\rho_{jk}=0 . \]

Control the false discovery rate with Benjamini–Hochberg at $q = 0.05$. Sort the $m = 1{,}770$ p-values $p_{(1)}\le\dots\le p_{(m)}$. Keep the edges with rank at most

\[ i^\ast = \max\{\, i : p_{(i)} \le iq/m \,\}. \]

Compare the two edge sets with

\[ J = \frac{|E_{\text{GGM}} \cap E_{\text{marg}}|}{|E_{\text{GGM}} \cup E_{\text{marg}}|},\qquad \text{explained-away share} = 1 - \frac{|E_{\text{GGM}} \cap E_{\text{marg}}|}{|E_{\text{marg}}|}. \]

The second number is the fraction of significant correlations that disappear after conditioning. Those are associations that run through other items rather than directly.

§5Centrality

Every measure is computed on $W = \hat\Omega$. Rankings are reported as z-scores across the 60 nodes.

5.1 Strength and expected influence

\begin{equation} s_j = \sum_{k\neq j} |\hat\omega_{jk}|, \qquad \mathrm{EI}_j = \sum_{k\neq j} \hat\omega_{jk}. \end{equation}

Strength ignores sign. Expected influence keeps it.

The two differ only at nodes with negative edges. Our likely candidates are E02 (exams measure knowledge) and E03 (compulsory attendance), since those are the items the class disagrees with. About 16.7% of the marginal Spearman correlations are negative.

5.2 Bridge strength

This measures how much a node connects to items outside its own theme (Jones, Ma & McNally, 2021):

\begin{equation} b_j = \sum_{k:\ \tau(k)\neq\tau(j)} |\hat\omega_{jk}|. \end{equation}

A high $b_j$ marks an item that ties the themes together. For example, T12 (AI regulation) might link Technology and Society.

5.3 Predictability

This is the share of item $j$'s variance explained by its neighbours $N(j) = \{k : \hat\omega_{jk}\neq 0\}$ (Haslbeck & Waldorp, 2018):

\begin{equation} R^2_j = \tilde r_{j,N(j)}^{\top}\ \tilde R_{N(j),N(j)}^{-1}\ \tilde r_{N(j),j}. \end{equation}

It is drawn as a ring around each node. A low $R^2_j$ means the item is driven by something outside the network. That is expected for the consensus items.

5.4 Closeness and betweenness (with a caveat)

Define edge length $\ell_{jk} = 1/|\hat\omega_{jk}|$. Let $d(j,k)$ be the shortest-path distance.

We use harmonic closeness because isolated nodes are likely:

\[ H_j = \frac{1}{p-1}\sum_{k\neq j}\frac{1}{d(j,k)}, \qquad B_j = \sum_{s\neq j\neq t}\frac{\sigma_{st}(j)}{\sigma_{st}} . \]

Here $\sigma_{st}$ counts shortest paths from $s$ to $t$, and $\sigma_{st}(j)$ counts those that pass through $j$. By convention $1/d = 0$ for disconnected pairs.

Caveat

Bringmann et al. (2019) argue that closeness and betweenness have no clear meaning in partial-correlation networks, and that they are usually unstable.

We report them only if their CS-coefficient (§7.3) passes. Strength, EI and bridge strength are the main results.

§6Communities and the theme test

6.1 Graph used for clustering

Modularity-based methods need non-negative weights. We cluster on the positive part only:

\[ A^+_{jk} = \max(\hat\omega_{jk}, 0),\qquad k_j = \sum_k A^+_{jk},\qquad 2m = \sum_j k_j . \]

Negative edges are kept for the plots and for EI. They are dropped only for clustering.

6.2 Modularity and Louvain

\begin{equation}\label{eq:Q} Q(c) = \frac{1}{2m}\sum_{j,k}\Big[A^+_{jk} - \gamma_r\frac{k_j k_k}{2m}\Big]\,\one[c_j = c_k]. \end{equation}

Louvain (Blondel et al., 2008) greedily maximizes $Q$. The resolution is $\gamma_r = 1$.

Louvain is randomized, so we run it with 100 seeds. We keep the partition with the highest $Q$. We also report how often each pair of items ends up together:

\[ C_{jk} = \frac{1}{100}\sum_{s=1}^{100}\one\big[c^{(s)}_j = c^{(s)}_k\big]. \]

6.3 Walktrap

Walktrap is the convention in Exploratory Graph Analysis (Golino & Epskamp, 2017), which uses GGM + walktrap for this exact kind of item network.

Take the random-walk transition matrix $P = D^{-1}A^+$ with $D = \operatorname{diag}(k)$ and $t = 4$ steps. The distance between two nodes is

\[ \rho_{jk} = \Big\| D^{-1/2}\big(P^t_{j\cdot} - P^t_{k\cdot}\big) \Big\|_2 . \]

Nodes are merged agglomeratively using a Ward-type criterion on $\rho$. The dendrogram is cut where $Q$ is maximal.

Decision D4 · algorithm

Use walktrap as the primary method, because it matches EGA and the papers we cite. Report Louvain agreement as a robustness check.

6.4 Do communities recover the themes?

Let $U$ be the found partition and $\mathcal T$ the theme partition. Let $n_{uv}$ be the number of items in community $u$ and theme $v$. Let $a_u = \sum_v n_{uv}$ and $b_v = \sum_u n_{uv}$.

Normalized mutual information (0 = unrelated, 1 = identical):

\begin{equation} \mathrm{NMI}(U,\mathcal T) = \frac{2\,I(U;\mathcal T)}{H(U)+H(\mathcal T)},\qquad I = \sum_{u,v}\frac{n_{uv}}{p}\log\frac{p\,n_{uv}}{a_u b_v}. \end{equation}

Adjusted Rand index (0 = chance level, 1 = identical; Hubert & Arabie, 1985):

\begin{equation} \mathrm{ARI} = \frac{\sum_{uv}\binom{n_{uv}}{2} - \big[\sum_u\binom{a_u}{2}\sum_v\binom{b_v}{2}\big]\big/\binom{p}{2}} {\tfrac12\big[\sum_u\binom{a_u}{2}+\sum_v\binom{b_v}{2}\big] - \big[\sum_u\binom{a_u}{2}\sum_v\binom{b_v}{2}\big]\big/\binom{p}{2}}. \end{equation}

6.5 Permutation test on the theme partition

NMI and ARI tell us how similar the partitions are. They don't tell us whether the themes are a significantly modular split of the network. For that:

  1. Compute $Q(\tau)$ with \eqref{eq:Q}, using the theme labels as the partition.
  2. Draw $B = 10{,}000$ random relabellings $\pi_b$. Each keeps four groups of 15.
  3. Compute $Q(\pi_b)$ for each.
\[ p_{\text{perm}} = \frac{1 + \#\{\, b : Q(\pi_b) \ge Q(\tau) \,\}}{1 + B}. \]

6.6 Theme block matrix

This is the mean absolute edge weight within and between themes:

\[ \bar W_{ab} = \frac{1}{N_{ab}}\sum_{\substack{j\in\tau^{-1}(a),\ k\in\tau^{-1}(b)\\ j\lt k \text{ if } a=b}} |\hat\omega_{jk}|,\qquad N_{aa} = \tbinom{15}{2} = 105,\quad N_{ab} = 15^2 = 225 . \]

A strong diagonal means the themes are internally coherent. Large off-diagonal cells show which themes are linked in students' minds.

§7Robustness

With $n = 91$ and $p = 60$, we must show which results are stable. Following Epskamp et al. (2018), there are three checks, plus a sensitivity grid.

7.1 Edge accuracy: nonparametric bootstrap

For $b = 1,\dots,B$ with $B = 1000$:

  1. Resample $n$ rows with replacement to get $X^{(b)}$. Missing entries come along with their rows.
  2. Rerun the full pipeline (§3 → §4, including the EBIC selection) to get $\hat\Omega^{(b)}$.

Then report, per edge:

\[ \mathrm{CI}_{jk} = \big[\,q_{0.025}(\hat\omega^{(b)}_{jk}),\ q_{0.975}(\hat\omega^{(b)}_{jk})\,\big],\qquad \pi_{jk} = \frac{1}{B}\sum_b \one\big[\hat\omega^{(b)}_{jk}\neq 0\big]. \]

$\pi_{jk}$ is how often the edge is selected. The lasso shrinks estimates toward zero, so these intervals describe sampling variability. They are not valid confidence intervals in the classical coverage sense.

7.2 Edge difference test

To claim "edge A is stronger than edge B", check whether the bootstrap interval of $\hat\omega^{(b)}_A - \hat\omega^{(b)}_B$ excludes zero. The same test works for node strength.

7.3 Centrality stability: case-dropping bootstrap

For drop fractions $f \in F = \{0.05, 0.10, \dots, 0.75\}$ and $b = 1,\dots,B$:

  1. Sample $\lfloor(1-f)\,n\rfloor$ rows without replacement.
  2. Re-estimate the network and compute a centrality vector $\mathbf c^{(b,f)}\in\R^{p}$.
  3. Correlate it with the full-sample vector: $r_{b,f} = \operatorname{corr}(\mathbf c^{(b,f)}, \mathbf c)$.

The correlation-stability coefficient is

\begin{equation} \mathrm{CS}(0.7) = \max\Big\{\, f\in F :\ \frac{1}{B}\sum_b \one[r_{b,f} \ge 0.7] \ge 0.95 \,\Big\}. \end{equation}

Rule of thumb: CS ≥ 0.25 is the minimum for interpreting a centrality ranking, and CS ≥ 0.5 is preferred.

We don't know yet where our data will land. With $n = 91$, closeness and betweenness failing the 0.25 cut would not be surprising.

§8Optional extension: Response Item Network (ResIN)

The item network compresses each item into one node. That hides whether the few people who disagree do so consistently across items. ResIN (Lüders, Carpentras & Quayle, 2024) splits each item into its answer options.

Construction

Each node is an item–answer pair. Using the $\kappa$-merged scale, that gives at most $4\times 60 = 240$ nodes:

\[ V_{\text{ResIN}} = \{(j,c) : c \text{ observed for item } j\},\qquad D_{i,(j,c)} = \one[\,x_{ij} = c\,]. \]

The edge weight is the $\varphi$ coefficient between two indicator columns, computed on respondents observed for both items:

\[ \varphi_{(j,c),(k,d)} = \frac{n_{11}n_{00} - n_{10}n_{01}}{\sqrt{n_{1\cdot}\,n_{0\cdot}\,n_{\cdot1}\,n_{\cdot0}}} . \]

Two rules apply:

  • Pairs from the same item ($j = k$) get no edge. Their answers are mutually exclusive, so the correlation is negative by construction.
  • Only positive weights are kept.

Lay out with a force-directed algorithm. The main axis of the layout then orders answer options from one attitude pole to the other.

Before implementing: check the exact edge definition and filtering in Lüders et al. against this sketch.

What it adds here

It shows whether "Disagree on T01", "Disagree on T05" and "Agree on T12" form a coherent AI-sceptic cluster.

In the item network, that minority is outvoted by the 79% who agree.
